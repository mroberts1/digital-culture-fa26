*This article covers research on looksmaxxing communities. For the term's origins and mainstream use, see [[Looksmaxxing 1|Looksmaxxing (1)]].*

**Looksmaxxing** (also spelled **looksmaxing**) is an internet slang term describing efforts to maximize physical attractiveness, particularly among young men seeking better romantic prospects or social standing. The term combines "looks" with "maxxing," meaning maximizing an attribute. Associated practices range from grooming, skincare, and exercise to cosmetic surgery and potentially harmful interventions. Although commonly presented as self-improvement, looksmaxxing has attracted scholarly criticism concerning how personal choices become organized around collective appearance standards and external approval.

### History and usage

The term originated on incel, or "involuntary celibate," message boards in the early 2010s and reached wider audiences through platforms such as TikTok in the early 2020s. Its mainstream usage includes grooming advice, transformation videos, and humorous content, alongside material connected to its original subcultural setting. This range makes it important to distinguish ordinary appearance management from the ideological frameworks promoted within particular communities.

Criminologists Anda Iulia Solea and Lisa Sugiura trace how looksmaxxing terminology and appearance-rating systems circulate between fringe forums and TikTok. They argue that presenting incel concepts through self-improvement advice can make misogynistic and racialized assumptions less recognizable. Their research identifies mechanisms of ideological circulation, rather than establishing that everyone using the term endorses those assumptions.

### Practices

Participants commonly distinguish softmaxxing, involving comparatively non-invasive changes such as skincare and hairstyling, from hardmaxxing, which includes invasive interventions such as cosmetic surgery. These categories describe community terminology rather than medically established levels of safety or effectiveness. Practices discussed under the looksmaxxing label also include hormone use, severe food restriction, and "bonesmashing," an attempt to alter facial structure through deliberate injury.

The distinction between these activities matters because treating them as equivalent can either exaggerate the dangers of ordinary grooming or minimize the risks of extreme interventions. The shared label does not establish that all participants follow the same methods.

### Appearance ratings

Rating systems help determine which physical changes communities consider desirable. The PSL scale, named after the forums PUAHate, Sluthate, and Lookism, ranks attractiveness through numerical scores and assessments of facial characteristics. Solea and Sugiura describe it as pseudoscientific, identifying selective scientific claims and racialized beauty ideals within its framework.

Their analysis challenges the apparent neutrality of measurement. A numerical rating can make a social judgment appear to be an objective discovery about someone's body. However, assigning a score does not establish the validity of the assumptions behind it. The scale can consequently give community preferences the authority of biological facts, making conformity appear necessary rather than negotiable.

### Peer evaluation and personal autonomy

A 2025 study by Michael Halpin and colleagues analyzed 8,072 comments collected from discussion and rating boards on one looksmaxxing website. The researchers found users evaluating features such as height, facial proportions, and brow ridges against [[Mogging|narrow masculine ideals]]. They describe how criticism presented as objective advice connected perceived physical shortcomings to recommendations for bodily alteration.

The study also identifies a contradictory standard: participants were encouraged to change their appearance but could be criticized when those changes appeared excessive or insufficiently masculine. Following advice therefore did not guarantee approval. Separately, the authors describe "masculine demoralisation," in which appearance-based humiliation positions participants as failed men. Their account shows how a community advertised as supportive can reinforce feelings of inadequacy.

Sociologist Ozan Félix Sousbois examines peer instruction through the concept of bro-pedagogy, which combines encouragement, shared knowledge, and aggressive criticism. Participants develop plans for "ascension," understood as improved romantic and social standing. Negative assessments can become reasons for further modification, while peer recognition helps validate the resulting decisions.

Sousbois identifies a tension between resistance and conformity: participants criticize a social order they believe disadvantages unattractive men, yet pursue recognition by approximating its preferred bodies. Read together, these studies suggest that looksmaxxing can expand someone's capacity to modify their appearance while strengthening dependence on external evaluation. The community helps define the defect, recommends its correction, and judges whether that correction succeeds. Personal control over bodily modification consequently does not necessarily extend to control over the standards determining its value.

### Health concerns and research limitations

Clinical social worker Jordyn Tovey distinguishes appearance practices that support confidence from compulsive behaviors that interfere with everyday life. She argues that presenting extreme appearance management as discipline or optimization can obscure psychological distress. This distinction locates concern in the relationship between behavior, distress, and daily functioning, rather than treating every effort to improve appearance as pathological.

The available research also has limits. Halpin and colleagues studied popular threads on one website and acknowledged that online accounts may be exaggerated or fabricated. Their findings cannot establish the prevalence of particular behaviors across all participants. They nevertheless document a mechanism through which the pursuit of self-improvement can make acceptance conditional on compliance with community standards.
