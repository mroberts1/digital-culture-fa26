title: Oversharing
---

**Oversharing** (/ˈōvərˌSHeriNG/;<sup><a id="cite-ref-1-a" data-router-ignore href="#cite-note-1">[1]</a></sup> first coined in the 1940s) is a term used to describe the action of someone sharing more information than is necessary or desirable. The earliest evidence for oversharing is from 1949, found from the Philadelphia Housing Quality Surv. In the twentieth century, oversharing is typically used to describe when two or more people are conversing about the details of one's personal life in an inappropriate manner (i.e. sharing details about one's sex life or talking about death) or in an inappropriate timeframe. The act of oversharing can put those listening in an uncomfortable position. It may also make others feel as though they are being used as a personal therapist. Oversharing has become a common thing amongst Gen Z and Gen Alpha on social media platforms such as TikTok and Instagram.

### Oversharing online

While oversharing has been a term used since the 1940s, its use is most frequently seen in the digital age. Oversharing online has become a dangerous phenomenon that has the potential to instigate identity theft, cyberbullying, and other security risks. There have also been studies to determine which gender group (out of boys versus girls) is prone to oversharing more, with boys coming out as the main culprit of this study.<sup><a id="cite-ref-2-a" data-router-ignore href="#cite-note-2">[2]</a></sup>

Often associated with oversharing in Gen Z and Gen Alpha is higher levels of anxiety, attention-seeking, and [[Brain rot|social media addiction]]. As these generations rely more and more on technology as their form of connection, oversharing becomes the result of the home they have built within these social mediascapes. This results in other people becoming the unwanted therapists as described in the section above, and can be tied to the attention seeking aspect of why people overshare. Many young adults and teenagers are left feeling more isolated since the age of Covid-19, causing them to confide in strangers online. The strangers, who could be anyone, taking this information and storing it for themselves with intentions that can be ill.

### Example

Harvard Business School talks about how the risks of oversharing online have impacted a young adult named Sam. This individual had posted about an upcoming trip they were excited about, sharing to their profile "Headed to Dubai with my whole team for a conference. Away from home and the office Jan 10-24. No email, just DM if you need me."<sup><a id="cite-ref-3-a" data-router-ignore href="#cite-note-3">[3]</a></sup> Not only did the individual share this statement, but also posted a photo of the flight boarding pass, tagged the hotel and posted a group selfie in front of the office sign. Just days after this post, Sam received a message from "your bank" which mentioned said trip and asked to confirm a suspicious charge.

Sam serves as an example of one of the many risks involved with oversharing information online and publicly. While oversharing may seem to pose no threats in the moment, its effects on the sharer and listener can be equally as negative. Oversharing affects people on both ends of the stick and should be considered before hitting the "post" button or thought through before speaking.
